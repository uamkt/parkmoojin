# KAS Part 21/23 Certification: BLIS-D's Path to Civil Aviation

## Abstract

On 20 March 1995, **Sarin** nerve agent released inside five Tokyo subway lines demonstrated with lethal clarity that civilian transportation infrastructure is not a bystander to CBRN events — it is a primary vector. Thirteen people died, nearly **1,000** required hospitalization, and the entire Kasumigaseki network was paralyzed for hours not by the agent itself, but by the absence of any rapid, platform-compatible decontamination capability. The aircraft industry has faced an analogous gap in silence: no airworthiness-certified, waterless decontamination system exists for civil aviation platforms, despite the sector's unmatched global reach and its role in medevac, VIP transport, and humanitarian logistics.

This article argues that **BLIS-D** — UAM KoreaTech's bleed-air dry decontamination system — has a viable, structured pathway to civil aviation deployment through Korean Airworthiness Standards **KAS Part 21** (type and supplemental type certification) and **KAS Part 23** (normal-category airplane airworthiness standards), both administered by South Korea's **Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT)**. Achieving this certification would create a dual-use capability: a commercially deployable, airworthiness-documented system that simultaneously satisfies the technical data requirements for NATO STANAG decontamination qualification. The strategic implications extend from hub airport resilience to allied force multiplication across the Indo-Pacific.

## 1. Historical Anchor — The Tokyo Subway Attack and the Absent Decon Layer

### Inner Landscape

Shoko Asahara and the operational planners of **Aum Shinrikyo** understood something that most emergency managers in 1995 did not: a mass-transit network is simultaneously a delivery system, a dispersal mechanism, and a trap. Asahara's inner logic, reconstructed through post-arrest interrogations and analyzed by RAND, reflected a theology of apocalyptic acceleration — the attack was intended to trigger government collapse, not merely cause casualties. What his planners did not model, and what the Japanese government was equally unprepared to confront, was the post-release phase: how do you decontaminate a subway car, a station platform, or the clothing of **5,510 affected commuters** in real time, without secondary responder casualties?

The Aum leadership assumed chaos would be self-sustaining. In that assessment, they were partially correct — but for the wrong reason. The chaos was not primarily caused by the agent's lethality. It was caused by the systemic absence of any rapid, infrastructure-compatible decontamination protocol.

### Environmental Read

Emergency responders in Tokyo arrived without adequate personal protective equipment, without agent-specific detection, and without any decontamination capability that could be applied at transit scale. The environmental factors Aum's planners missed — and that the Japanese government had equally failed to anticipate — included the condensed dwell time of passengers, the ventilation patterns of underground networks, and the fact that the nearest hospital, St. Luke's International, would receive **640 patients within the first hour**, many of whom became secondary contamination sources. The absence of a rapid decon layer at the point of exposure meant that the contamination event propagated outward through the civilian infrastructure rather than being contained at source.

Transportation nodes — subway cars, airport terminals, aircraft cabins — share a critical geometric property: they concentrate people, recirculate air, and connect rapidly to secondary networks. This geometry did not change between 1995 and today.

### Differential Factor

What distinguished the Tokyo attack from prior chemical incidents was scale of infrastructure penetration combined with the absence of any military-style decontamination asset positioned near civilian nodes. Industrial accident protocols of the era assumed stationary sources and open-air dispersal. The subway attack proved that enclosed, mobile infrastructure required a categorically different decontamination approach — one that was fast, dry (to avoid secondary contamination runoff), and operable by personnel without extensive CBRN training.

Thirty years later, that decontamination approach does not yet exist in certified form for civil aviation platforms. **BLIS-D** is designed to close that gap.

### Modern Bridge

The tactical lesson of 1995 translates directly to 2026's civil aviation environment. A single confirmed chemical or biological contamination event aboard a commercial aircraft at a hub airport — Incheon, Frankfurt, Dubai — would trigger a multi-day grounding of the affected aircraft, potentially cascading into fleet groundings pending investigation. No airworthiness-certified rapid decontamination system currently exists to break that cascade. MOLIT's **KAS Part 21/23** framework, already harmonized with ICAO Annex 8 airworthiness principles, provides the legal and technical architecture to certify exactly such a system. For UAM KoreaTech, the regulatory pathway is not a bureaucratic obstacle — it is a moat.

## 2. Problem Definition — The Certification Gap in Civil Aviation CBRN Decon

The global CBRN defense market was valued at approximately **USD 16.3 billion in 2023** and is projected to reach **USD 22.8 billion by 2029**, according to MarketsandMarkets, driven substantially by dual-use and homeland security procurement. Within this market, aviation-platform decontamination represents one of the least-addressed segments: there is no commercially available, airworthiness-certified chemical or biological decontamination system designed for installation on fixed-wing civil aircraft.

The consequences of this gap are measurable. Following the **2018 Salisbury Novichok** incident, UK authorities required **approximately 11 days** to decontaminate the sites of exposure. While that incident did not involve aircraft, modeling by the UK Cabinet Office has indicated that an equivalent nerve-agent exposure aboard a commercial aircraft would require airframe quarantine periods of similar or greater duration under current protocols, given the absence of a certified onboard or ground-based rapid decon capability compatible with aircraft materials and pneumatic systems.

From an airworthiness perspective, the barrier is not purely technical — **BLIS-D**'s bleed-air thermal process has been engineered to operate within the pressure and temperature tolerances of existing aircraft pneumatic systems. The barrier is regulatory documentation. **KAS Part 21** requires a complete type or supplemental type certification (STC) package, including materials compatibility testing, failure mode analysis, and thermal performance data across the operational envelope. **KAS Part 23** adds airworthiness standards for structural integration and system independence. Building this documentation package is a **12-24 month** engineering and regulatory engagement with **MOLIT** — resource-intensive, but finite and achievable.

For NATO CBRN procurement officers, the absence of this certification represents a force-multiplication gap: government-operated civil aircraft that serve medevac, VIP, and disaster relief roles cannot be rapidly returned to service after a contamination event, effectively removing them from the response inventory at exactly the moment they are needed most.

## 3. UAM KoreaTech Solution — BLIS-D's Dual-Use Certification Strategy

**BLIS-D** (Bleed-air Liquid-In-Solid Decontamination) operates on a principle that is architecturally native to fixed-wing aircraft: it draws from the aircraft's existing bleed-air pneumatic supply to drive a thermal-dry decontamination cycle, completing full chemical and biological surface decontamination in approximately **90 seconds** with no aqueous waste stream. This is not an adaptation of a ground-based system — it is a system whose energy source is the aircraft itself.

The KAS Part 21 Supplemental Type Certificate pathway allows **BLIS-D** to be certified as an installed modification on an existing type-certificated aircraft, rather than requiring a new type certificate from scratch. This dramatically reduces the certification timeline and cost, mirroring the FAA STC process familiar to international operators. Under **KAS Part 23**, the system must demonstrate compliance with structural load requirements, pneumatic-system interface tolerances, and functional independence — ensuring that a **BLIS-D** activation cannot compromise primary flight systems. UAM KoreaTech's engineering architecture has been designed from initial specification to satisfy these constraints, with pneumatic draw rates calibrated to remain within the bleed-air budget of the target platform class.

The dual-use dividend is significant: a **KAS Part 21**-certified **BLIS-D** installation produces a technical data package — thermal performance curves, materials compatibility matrices, failure mode and effects analysis — that directly satisfies the evidentiary requirements for NATO STANAG decontamination qualification testing. An allied nation procuring **BLIS-D** for government aviation platforms receives not only a certified system but a pre-structured NATO qualification dossier, reducing allied procurement timelines by an estimated **6-9 months** compared to a non-certified competitor system.
